# Selling strategies

Good salespeople do not all sell the same way, and neither do good sites. Each strong selling site has a deliberate manner, and its whole interface is built to carry that manner out. This reference names thirteen such strategies, says when each fits, what it looks like when done well, and how it fails.

Read this for any surface whose job is to sell, sign up or win a lead, together with [patterns/sales-journey.md](patterns/sales-journey.md). Use it to answer three questions, in order:

1. **Which strategy is this surface attempting?** Record it in the classification.
2. **Does that strategy suit what is being sold and who is buying?**
3. **Does the interface carry the strategy out, or get in its way?** Most findings come from here.

## Rules for using this catalog

- **The strategy is the owner's.** Identify it, judge the site against it, and help it execute. Do not swap it.
- **A different strategy is a recommendation, never a fix.** If the fit is poor, or no strategy is discernible, say which would suit and why, and leave the decision with the owner.
- **Examples are evidence, not templates.** Never restyle a site toward a named brand. The brand-preservation rules hold.
- **A strategy cannot be built from nothing.** Storytelling needs real imagery, proof-first needs real proof, try-it-now needs a product that can be tried. When the material does not exist, list what the owner must supply. Do not invent it.
- **One lead strategy per page; others may serve a stage.** Strong sites combine them: proof at the answer stage, a quiz for guiding, a configurator at the close. That is normal. The problem is a page with no lead manner, where every section sells a different way.
- **Naming a site's strategy is a judgment.** Say so, give the signals you read it from, and state your confidence. Where two readings are plausible, name both and check against the one the first screen supports.
- **Read the strategy from the site, not from one page.** A pricing page, product page or checkout is a stage of the site's strategy. For such a page on its own, record the strategy as "part of the close; site strategy not visible" and apply `patterns/sales-journey`.
- **When the owner names a brand** ("make mine sell like Apple"): do not restyle. Say which strategy that brand uses, check it against the fit table for this offer, pull out the habits that transfer (for example: keep the action in view, show the product, one idea per screen), map them to findings, list the material the strategy would need, and ask the owner whether they want to adopt it.
- **None of these promises sales.** Each removes particular reasons to leave. Report those.

## Identifying the strategy

Look at the first screen, the main action, what repeats down the page, and what the page spends its length on.

| If the page mostly… | It is probably attempting |
|---|---|
| Shows the product in large imagery and video, one idea per screen, for a very long scroll | 1. Immersive storytelling |
| Asks the visitor a question and offers to choose for them | 2. Guided choice |
| Shows products in a calm grid with almost nothing else | 3. Quiet catalog |
| Stacks evidence of scale, customers and capability, opening detail on demand | 4. Authority by depth |
| Is spare and fast, and shows the product's own interface as the picture | 5. The site is the demo |
| Pushes one free action to start using the product now | 6. Try it now |
| Leads with ratings, numbers, customers or endorsements | 7. Proof first |
| Leads with a price or a deal | 8. Offer first |
| Makes an argument in sentences, in a distinct voice | 9. The letter |
| Walks the visitor through assembling their own version | 10. Build your own |
| Puts a search or request form where the pitch would be | 11. Task first |
| Shows past work and invites a conversation | 12. Portfolio and conversation |
| Says plainly what is offered, what it costs and how to get it, and little else | 13. Plain dealing |

If nothing fits, say so and audit with `patterns/sales-journey` alone. A missing strategy is worth reporting only when the page sells in several clashing ways, or sells nothing in particular; a small, clear page is usually strategy 13.

## Which strategy fits what

| What is sold and to whom | Strategies that usually fit |
|---|---|
| A product people must want before they compare: premium, visual, physical | 1, with 10 for the purchase |
| Many variants, and a buyer unsure which is theirs | 2 |
| A simple product in a familiar category, bought on a phone, often repeated | 3, with 8 in sale periods |
| Complex, invisible or high-risk products bought by organizations | 4, with 7 |
| Tools for expert users where speed and craft are the value | 5, with 6 |
| Software with a free start and quick first result | 6 |
| An unknown brand, a considered purchase, or a service where trust is the barrier | 7 |
| A commodity where price decides | 8 |
| An opinionated product in a crowded market | 9 |
| A product the buyer configures | 10 |
| A marketplace or utility the visitor arrives at with a task in mind | 11 |
| Bespoke services sold through a relationship | 12, with 7 |
| A small or local business with a simple, well-understood offer | 13, with 7 |

## The strategies

For each: the selling logic, what it looked like on the sites observed (October 2026, phone-sized screen; SURVEY-02), what to check, and how it fails. "Seen on" names sites whose pages showed the pattern when measured; assigning a site to a strategy is this skill's reading.

### 1. Immersive storytelling
**Logic:** make the visitor want the thing before they think about the price. One idea per screen, shown, not told.
**Seen on:** Apple product pages, Rivian R1S, Tesla Model 3, Sonos. Pages of 15 to 42 screens; a first screen that is 35% to 85% image or video with 7 to 35 words; 8 to 26 videos; a slim bar that keeps the product name and a Buy button in view.
**Check:**
- The first screen names the product and its main promise in words, not only in an image.
- A way to buy stays visible for the whole scroll, and the price appears early.
- Each screen makes one point, headed by a line that works without the animation.
- Scrolling behaves normally; where it is altered, the stretch is short and holds no essential text.
- The page stays usable while media loads, with reduced motion, and on a slow phone.
**Fails when:** the story is so heavy the page takes many seconds to show anything (observed: 7 to 22 MB on a phone); the visitor cannot find the price or specifications without finishing the scroll; the only Buy button is at the top.

### 2. Guided choice
**Logic:** remove the work of choosing. Ask a few easy questions, then recommend.
**Seen on:** Warby Parker ("Start with a quiz" beside "Shop eyeglasses"), Function of Beauty, Casper, Noom, Lemonade, Duolingo. First screens of about 12 to 20 words with one dominant action that starts the guided flow.
**Check:**
- The guided route is offered on the first screen, next to a direct route for visitors who already know what they want.
- Questions are few, one at a time, in plain words, with progress shown and Back working.
- The visitor is told how long it takes and what they get at the end.
- The result explains why it was recommended, shows the price, and can be changed.
- Contact details are not demanded before the result.
**Fails when:** the quiz is a lead-capture form in disguise; the result is the same for everyone; there is no way to browse without it; answers are lost on Back.

### 3. Quiet catalog
**Logic:** the product sells itself; the interface gets out of the way.
**Seen on:** Allbirds, Glossier, Everlane, Patagonia, Harry's. Short home pages (4.5 to 7 screens), 6 to 9 text sizes, 4 or 5 text colors, a first screen that is mostly one photograph, add-to-cart available from the listing, and on the product page a slim bottom bar with name, price and one button.
**Check:**
- The first screen shows what is sold and gives routes by the way people shop (men, women, category).
- Listings show enough to choose and act: image, name, price, variants.
- The product page has the price, options and the buy action in the first screen on a phone.
- Typography, color and spacing are few and consistent; nothing competes with the product.
- Delivery and return terms are stated near the buy action.
**Fails when:** restraint removes information buyers need (sizes, materials, delivery); routes are hidden behind an unlabeled icon; a region or newsletter dialog covers the first screen of every page (observed on one otherwise exemplary store).

### 4. Authority by depth
**Logic:** for something invisible or risky, show that serious organizations already rely on it, and let each reader go as deep as they need.
**Seen on:** Stripe, Plaid, Twilio, Cloudflare. Long pages (12 to 24 screens) dense with named customers, figures and product breadth; two starting actions (start now, talk to sales); detail opened on demand.
**Check:**
- The first screen says what the product is in plain words; scale claims support it and are real.
- Summary first, detail on request: each layer is complete on its own.
- Different readers (developer, buyer, executive) each find their route from the first screen or the menu.
- Customer names, numbers and certifications are current, attributed and linked where possible.
- A self-serve start and a sales route are both offered, with pricing reachable.
**Fails when:** visual richness slows or distracts (observed: over 80 animations on one page); the headline is abstract and the product is never stated; depth is gated behind a form.

### 5. The site is the demo
**Logic:** the site behaves like the product: fast, precise, spare. Its quality is the argument.
**Seen on:** Linear, Vercel, Resend, Raycast, Cursor. Pages of 7 to 19 screens, as few as 12 words per screen, two to four navigation items, one start action, and the product's real interface as the main picture.
**Check:**
- The headline still says what the product does and for whom; minimal is not vague.
- The product is shown truthfully: a real screen, not an abstraction.
- The site is measurably fast and stable; any lag contradicts the pitch.
- Contrast and target sizes survive the dark, low-key styling.
- Pricing and documentation are one step away.
**Fails when:** the headline is a slogan a newcomer cannot decode (observed several times); minimal navigation hides pricing; low-contrast text; the page is slower than its claim.

### 6. Try it now
**Logic:** the fastest way to believe a product is to use it. Shorten the distance to first use.
**Seen on:** Canva, Figma, Notion, Grammarly, Calendly, Loom, Typeform, Duolingo. A free start on the first screen of all eight; sign-in shortcuts on the first screen of three; the same start action repeated two or three times down a short page; customer logos directly under the hero.
**Check:**
- The start action is on the first screen, says it is free where it is, and says whether a card is needed.
- The first step after the click is the product or the shortest possible sign-up, not a long form.
- Value is visible before sign-up where possible: an example, a template, a live demo.
- The page still explains what the product is for people not ready to try.
- What is free and what is paid is stated plainly.
**Fails when:** "free" hides a card requirement or a trial that converts silently; sign-up comes before any value; the page says nothing about what the product does.

### 7. Proof first
**Logic:** when trust is the barrier, lead with other people's evidence.
**Seen on:** Thumbtack (rating, review count and number of professionals directly under the search box), Toptal, Ridge (a trial-period bar above the header), Dyson, Grammarly, HEY (two dozen named quotations), Casper.
**Check:**
- Proof is specific and attributed: who, how many, measured how, as of when.
- It sits beside the claim and the action it supports, starting on or just below the first screen.
- Negative reviews are not filtered out; ratings show their count.
- Guarantees and trial terms state their conditions.
- Every figure, name, logo and quotation can be verified from the project or the owner.
**Fails when:** proof is generic, undated or unverifiable; a wall of logos replaces an explanation of the product; proof is invented or borrowed. Unverifiable proof that carries the sale (testimonials, ratings, results, client names) is at least P1; an incidental unverified detail is lower. Having no proof at all on an offer where trust is the barrier is also P1.

### 8. Offer first
**Logic:** when price decides, say the price first and say it loudly.
**Seen on:** Mint Mobile (the monthly price is the largest thing on the first screen), and seasonal sale banners leading the first screen at Casper, Ridge, Everlane and Function of Beauty.
**Check:**
- The headline price is the price charged, with period, minimum term and conditions beside it, not only in small print below.
- A discount states its former price, its end date and what it applies to.
- The route from the offer to buying is one step.
- The page stays calm: an offer does not need many sizes and colors (one offer-led page used 22 text sizes).
- All of `patterns/pricing-and-persuasion` applies.
**Fails when:** the big number is not what the visitor will pay; urgency is permanent; conditions are hidden; the deal shouts over everything else on the page.

### 9. The letter
**Logic:** win the reader with an argument in a human voice. People who agree with the reasoning buy the product.
**Seen on:** Basecamp (about 290 words on the first screen), HEY, Lemonade. Roughly 115 to 145 words per screen, well above the 79 median, with a slim bottom bar keeping the start action in view.
**Check:**
- The first lines state the problem and the position; a skimmer gets the argument from the headings alone.
- Long text is typeset for reading: comfortable size, measured line length, short paragraphs.
- The start action stays reachable throughout.
- The voice is consistent and suits the stakes; trust outweighs wit.
- Claims are backed by the proof that follows.
**Fails when:** the wall of text has no structure; the voice turns smug or jokes about the buyer's money; the action appears only after the last paragraph.

### 10. Build your own
**Logic:** a buyer who assembles their own version has already half-decided.
**Seen on:** Apple's buy pages (step by step: model, then size, then options, with the price from the first step), Rivian ("Design yours" repeated five times on the product page), Tesla.
**Check:**
- Steps come one at a time in a sensible order, each with a good default.
- The running price updates visibly with every choice and is always on screen.
- Each option says what it changes and what it costs; differences are comparable.
- Choices survive going back and leaving; a summary precedes the commitment.
- The preview reflects the choices truthfully.
**Fails when:** the price appears only at the end; a choice silently resets others; the dearest options are pre-selected; the configuration is lost on reload.

### 11. Task first
**Logic:** the visitor arrived with a task. Let them start it; the result is the pitch.
**Seen on:** Booking.com (three fields and Search fill the first screen), Airbnb, Uber, OpenTable, Thumbtack, Wise. A form on the first screen; short pages (4 to 8 screens); reassurance in a few lines under the form; on phones, a bottom tab bar.
**Check:**
- The form is the first thing on the screen, with as few fields as will produce a result, and sensible defaults.
- Inputs suit a phone: right keyboards, location shortcuts, forgiving dates.
- Results answer the task: price with fees, availability, the next step.
- Reassurance (cancellation, coverage, ratings) sits beside the form, not above it.
- Nothing is asked for before results that is not needed to produce them.
**Fails when:** an account or app download is demanded before results; a pop-up covers the form; results hide fees or availability.

### 12. Portfolio and conversation
**Logic:** bespoke services are bought from people. Show the work, show who you are, make it easy to start talking.
**Seen on:** Pentagram (about 240 images of work, filtered by a sentence the visitor completes), IDEO ("Say hello" in the header), Toptal (a switch between two kinds of client on the first screen, and one hiring action repeated).
**Check:**
- The first screen says what the firm does and for whom, in a sentence, not only an image of past work.
- Work is browsable by what the visitor needs (sector, service), and each piece says what was done and what resulted.
- The people and the process are visible.
- A way to start the conversation is always in reach, says what happens next and how soon, and asks little.
- A price range, typical engagement or starting point is given where the business allows.
**Fails when:** the site is a gallery with no statement of services, or the reverse, services with no work or people shown; contact is a bare email link; nothing indicates cost, timing or process; the work has no captions.

### 13. Plain dealing
**Logic:** say what you do, what it costs and how to get it. For a simple, familiar offer, clarity is the whole pitch.
**Seen on:** not in the survey, which covered large brands. This is the natural manner of most small-business, local-service and single-page sites, and it is included so that such sites are judged against what they are trying to be (evidence class: Judgment).
**Check:**
- The first screen states the offer, who it is for and where, with the main action.
- Prices or a price range are shown, with what is included.
- The business is identifiable: name, place, people, a way to reach a human.
- Some real evidence that others have been served is present, however modest.
- The action is short and says what happens next and when.
**Fails when:** plainness becomes anonymity (no name, address or proof); the price is clear but what the button commits the visitor to is not; nothing distinguishes the business from any other.

## Reporting

In the classification, add one line: *Selling strategy: attempted (and any serving a single stage), the signals it was read from, your confidence, and whether it suits the offer.* In the findings, tie each sales finding to the stage of the sales journey it harms and, where relevant, to the strategy check it fails. Put any recommendation to change or adopt a strategy under functional recommendations, with what the owner would need to supply.
